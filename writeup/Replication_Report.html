Introduction

Emotion is an embodied experience, involving interaction between mind and body (Barrett & Lindquist, 2008; Damasio & Carvalho, 2013). Contemporary models of emotion diverge in whether the bodily changes associated with emotions follow a universal pattern (Barrett et al., 2006; Kreibig, 2010; Volynets et al., 2020). A series of research explored universality of how people associate different emotions with bodily sensations across 15 countries (Nummenmaa et al., 2014; Volynets et al., 2020). In particular, for the replication project, I focus on the experiment 1c from Nummenmaa et al. (2014), where they conducted a comparison between two different cultural groups: Finnish-Taiwanese (N=36). These groups differ more substantially in both cultural and linguistic distance than the two groups in experiment 1b (Finnish-Swedish,) and the small sample size makes the replication feasible within the short time frame. However, recruiting Finnish and Taiwanese participants is challenging, and our research team lacks sufficient language proficiency to interpret responses from these groups with confidence. To address this, I propose to conceptually replicate the study using two culturally distant populations that are more accessible: native English-speaking Americans and Koreans.

Nummenmaa et al. (2014) included two independent variables - the type of emotion words (basic vs nonbasic; measured within-subject), and the culture group (Finnish vs Taiwanese; measured between-subject). For replicating experiment 1c, we focused on the second variable, the culture group differences, specifically, whether the Spearman correlation between embodied emotion representation of two participants from different cultures are larger within the basic emotion categories (matched condition; anger-Finnish vs anger-Taiwanese) than that of between-category (mismatched condition; anger-Finnish vs happiness-Taiwanese).The effect size between the two conditions and the variability measures are not reported in the study, and the data of the original paper was not accessible to the original paper’s authors. Therefore, the replication objective was to replicating the correlation values of the two conditions.

In addition, we conducted exploratory analyses extending beyond the original study. Specifically, we applied the same procedures to within-cultural participant pairs (i.e., two native Korean-speaking participants or two native English-speaking participants). Using the correlation values from matched and mismatched emotion conditions in these within-cultural pairs, we also explored differences between within-culture and between-culture similarity patterns.

One anticipated challenge in running this experiment on Prolific is accurately identifying participants’ cultural backgrounds. Especially for Korean speakers recruited through prolific, it is highly likely that they are equally proficient in English or more familiar with the Western cultural context. To mitigate this, a language background survey will be attached, in addition to the body representation task, so that we can accurately identify the target culture group.

Methods

Reliability and Validity

The experiment that I plan to replicate (experiment 1c) did not report any reliability or validity related measures. However, experiment 1b and experiment 1c were attemps to measure whether the trend observed in experiment 1 generalizes to other cultures, especially for the basic emotions. Therefore, the experiment 1c itself is an attempt to check the external validity.

Power Analysis

Power analysis was not feasible because the original paper did not report the effect size, nor the original data was accessible. Therefore, this replication closely followed the sample size of the original experiment.

Planned Sample

Following the sample size in Nummenmaa et al. (2014), 36 participants were collected, with 18 participants in each cultural group (United States, Korea.) To recruit two separate culture group, two separate prolific studies were created. To filter Korean participants, First Language-Korean, Primary Language-Korean, and Nationality-Korean filters were applied in prolific. To filter native English speakers from USA, Primary Language-English, First Language-English, and Nationality-United States filters were applied in prolific. Although there are only a small number of eligible Korean participants, recruiting 18 participants is practically feasible.

Since the estimated completion time is around 10 minutes, the compensation will be 1/6 of the average between the minimum California hourly wage and the federal hourly rage ($12/hour in average): each participant were be paid $2.00.

Materials

To measure the embodied representation of emotion, “Bodily Sensation Maps (BSM)” will be used, where participants will color body regions where they feel increased or decreased bodily activity for each emotion word presented. Six basic (anger, fear, disgust, happiness, sadness, and surprise) emotions, as well as a neutral state, will be presented. Web-based version of the BSM measure was created using the code provided in their repository (link), along with some minor changes wordings of the instructions. Unlike the original version, I also modified the code so that drawing outside of the body silhouette was not allowed or stored as data. Two versions of the experiment (one for the Korean speaking participants, and one for English speaking participants) were created, as experiment 1c also translated their materials into the respective languages that each cultural group speaks. Everything else remains the same with the original experiment, including the brush size, and the exact red and blue colors for indicating activation and deactivation, and the randomization of word orders.

In addition to the BSM measure, language background survey was attached at the end to accurately identify the target culture group. For assessing the language background, subscales from the Language Experience and Proficiency Questionnaire (LEAP-Q, Marian et al., 2007) will be used. The scales include the number of languages the participants speak, the order of acquisition and dominance of each languages they can speak, self-rated proficiency, and the context of usage of the listed languages.

Procedure

Participants were presented with two silhouettes of bodies alongside one of the seven emotion words (six basic emotions or neutral), and was asked to color the bodily regions were they felt activated or deactivated when feeling the given emotion. Then, they will fill out the language background survey.

Analysis Plan

The resulting data from Bodily Sensation Maps (BSM) measure were stored in a separate server, with each response as a group of coordinates indicating the participants’ brush touch. Using the preprocessing code provided by the authors, these groups of coordinates were converted to matrices storing count information in each pixel. Data from both bodies (the left body indicating activation, the right body indicating deactivation) consisted of 50,364 pixels each, with pixels only inside the body were calculated.

Exclusion criteria were attention check failures, trial completion time, and the drawing pattern shown in the BSM data. First, if the data points from participants failing any of the two attention checks were excluded. Moreover, data points from participants who had more than one trial completed in less than 5 seconds were excluded. Lastly, by visualizing each participant’s responses for each emotion, drawings exhibiting the similar response across 70% or more of the conditions (5 emotions out of 7) in both bodies, or exhibiting significant overlap in deactivation and activation responses in more than one condition, were excluded from the final analyses.

Following Nummenmaa et al. (2014), the similarity between the two emotion-wise BSMs from two participants were measured through pixel-wise correlation (Spearman’s rank correlation). Zero values in the heatmaps (i.e., regions without paint) were filled with Gaussian noise to avoid inflated correlations. Pixels outside the body silhouette were masked and not considered for analysis. For every possible cross-cultural participant pair (i.e., one native Korean and one native English-speaking participant), correlations for both matched-emotion BSMs (e.g., sadness–sadness) and mismatched-emotion BSMs (e.g., sadness–anger) were computed. Although we initially planned to compare the resulting correlation distributions using a t-test to evaluate differences between matched and mismatched conditions, we ultimately did not conduct this test because the correlations in both conditions were negligibly small. Although some details of the analysis pipeline were not fully specified in the original paper, the procedure described here is inferred from the results of Experiment 1c.

Differences from Original Study

In this conceptual replication, we recruited Korean participants and native English speakers from USA, due to the challenge in recruiting Finnish and Taiwanese participants through prolific, and our research team lacking sufficient language proficiency to interpret responses from these groups with confidence. It is highly likely that the Korean participants recruited through prolific are as equally proficient in English or more familiar to Western cultural context. It could be expected that the differences between within and between culture correlation will be smaller in the replication sample than that of the data from Nummenmaa et al. (2014).

Moreover, stricter exclusion criteria were contrived, given that this replication was conducted online. It is unclear whether the original experiment was conducted in-person or online, but since the original study used manual inspection to remove anomalous painting behavior, this project applies stricter criteria beyond the manual inspection for clearer criteria.

Confirmatory Analysis

For confirmatory analysis, pixel-wise Spearman correlations will be computed. First, list of of all possible pairs of two participants were collected. Then, for each pair of participants, correlations of the BSMs of all possible pairs of mismatched emotions (for example, sadness and anger) and matched emotions (for example, sadness and sadness) were computed and stored. Here, the correlations are computed only among valid pixels, which means that the ‘masked’ coordinates were used for analysis. Moreover, for uncolored pixels within the body silhouette, Gaussian noise were filled instead of 0 values to prevent inflating the correlations.

Discussion

Summary of Replication Attempt

The confimatory analysis showed close-to-zero mean correlation values in both of the conditions (r=0.044 for different culture same emotion condition, and r=0.028 for different culture different emotion condition). The values themselves are very different from the correlations reported in the original study (r=0.7 for different culture same emotion condition, and r=0.4), nor the difference between the two conditions are significant enough to require a statistical test. Therefore, we failed to replicate the original result.

Exploratory Analysis

We conducted to exploratory analysis. First, calculated mean correlation values for within culture condition to compare the correlation differences across between-and within-culture conditions. However, the two conditions also showed close-to-zero mean correlation values (r=0.042 for same culture same emotion condition, and r=0.027 for same culture different emotion condition). The differences between the two conditions are also not significant enough to require a statistical test.

Second, we also examined the distribution of correlation values across four conditions. All of the correlation showed wide range of variations with normal-like distribution and most of the values being close to zero.

Commentary

These results, altogether, could suggest more emotionwise/participantwise variations in this data sample to the original sample. Indeed, the visualization of BSMs suggest some possible cross-cultural differences for emotions like disgust, sadness, and surprise. Another possibility is that this replication attempt might have ommitted some procedures in processing the data for the main analyses, as the methods for the particular experiment we attempted to replicate (experiment 1c) was described vaguely and quite short in the original paper.
